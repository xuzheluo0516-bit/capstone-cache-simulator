# Shared Web Cache Simulator: Design and Experimental Evaluation

**F26 Introduction to Computer Science capstone | Five-person team | 27 November 2026**

## 1. System and policy

We built an offline simulator of a shared web cache serving multiple clients. It reads a resource catalogue (URL key, byte size, origin latency) and a time-ordered request trace, then emits per-request cache events and global and per-client statistics. The parser identifies a resource by `(scheme, host, path)` and validates CSV fields; equal-timestamp requests retain input order. The shared engine owns byte-capacity accounting, repeated eviction until an incoming object fits, and bypass of objects larger than total capacity. Every comparison starts with an empty cache. FIFO evicts the earliest insertion, LRU the least recently accessed object, and LFU the least frequently accessed resident object with recency tie-breaking. GDSF is a size-aware aging baseline.

Our custom policy, RW-CSF (`CUSTOM`), seeks to reduce **mean response time**. For resident object `i` at request step `n`, its retention score is `P_i(n) = F_i(n) * max(origin_latency_i - 5 ms, 0) / size_i`. `F_i` is an exponentially decayed access count: an observation's weight halves every `H` requests. The lowest score is evicted; ties use recency and then resource key. A zero-byte object cannot release space and is handled specially. The strategy records one frequency/last-step entry per resident object; the engine remains responsible for residency. RW-CSF always admits a fitting object, so a large insertion can still displace small objects before the strategy later evicts it. The policy does not use client identity and thus does not optimize fairness directly. [Design](custom-policy-design.md)

We validated FIFO, LRU, and LFU against all ten columns of the instructor's 15-request reference trace at 102,400 B capacity and 5 ms hit latency. The observed hit counts were 3, 3, and 4; average response times were 95.33, 92.33, and 84.33 ms. This checks implementation semantics, not the ranking on larger workloads. [Reference outputs](../data/reference/) [Validation](report-standard-policies.md)

## 2. Experimental method

The formal comparison uses five policies, three seeded synthetic workloads, five capacities (32, 128, 256, 512, 1,024 KiB), and five independent seeds (`20261111`–`20261115`): **375 main runs**. Each trace has 1,440 requests. `repeated` concentrates requests on a small hot set; `sequential` mostly visits new objects from a pool of at least 60, with 12.5% revisits; `mixed` combines browser, media, and random clients. Its 384 KiB object enters a 512 KiB cache and creates real eviction pressure; its 44 MiB video is deliberately too large to cache and occurs in a fixed prefix and about once per 100 later requests. Within each workload/seed condition, every policy reads the same resource and request CSV. All requests count, with no warm-up, and cache-hit latency is fixed at 5 ms. [Workload design](experiments.md)

We selected RW-CSF's half-life before formal evaluation using only tuning seed `31415` and candidates `H = 5, 20, 100, 500`. The pre-set response-time rule selected `H = 20`; no formal seed was used for tuning. For each seed and condition, we calculated request hit rate (`hits / requests`), byte hit rate (`hit bytes / requested bytes`), mean response time (request-weighted), origin traffic (sum of missed resource bytes), and per-client values. The tables report means and sample standard deviations across five seed-level measurements, rather than pooling requests across seeds. The 102,400 B reference capacity is a separate mandatory test, not a formal grid point. [Metric definitions](report-input-metrics.md)

## 3. Main results and trade-offs

**The best strategy depends on workload, capacity, and metric.** At 128 KiB on `repeated`, RW-CSF had the lowest mean response time (19.65 ms), ahead of GDSF (19.74), LFU (19.75), LRU (21.12), and FIFO (22.90). At 256 and 512 KiB, GDSF led response time (13.12 and 10.58 ms), ahead of RW-CSF (13.45 and 10.85 ms). At 1,024 KiB all five policies converged to 5.89 ms because the active cacheable set fits. These differences are means over five seeds; we did not conduct a significance test. [Main table](../data/results/main/main_table.csv)

For `sequential`, low reuse makes all policies miss most requests. At 1,024 KiB RW-CSF had the lowest mean response time (157.79 ms) and a 1.19% request hit rate, but the differences were small in absolute terms: FIFO was 159.07 ms with a 0.53% hit rate. LFU, not RW-CSF, had the lowest origin traffic (132.38 versus 132.55 MiB). This is one case where improving response time does not also minimize network traffic.

The clearest trade-off appears in `mixed` at 512 KiB. GDSF had the lowest mean response time (73.28 ms), with RW-CSF close behind (73.97); LFU took 75.33 ms. GDSF and RW-CSF had request hit rates of 64.29% and 64.24%, far above LFU's 45.58%, yet LFU had the lowest origin traffic: 708.99 MiB versus 751.55 MiB for GDSF and 755.75 MiB for RW-CSF. LFU also had the highest byte hit rate (15.41%). Thus a higher request hit rate does **not** guarantee less origin traffic, and the minimum mean latency does **not** imply minimum traffic. The recurring oversized video dominates byte totals and must be considered when reading these values. At 1,024 KiB the five strategies again converge in this workload. [Main table](../data/results/main/main_table.csv) [Mixed event evidence](mixed-workload-competition.md)

## 4. Client effects, ablation, and supplementary drift test

At 512 KiB in `mixed`, RW-CSF improves the browser client's mean latency from 36.43 ms under LRU to 7.54 ms, and the random client's from 81.56 to 65.31 ms. The media client worsens from 127.06 to 148.37 ms. GDSF shows a similar pattern; LFU serves media faster (109.57 ms) but sacrifices request hits for the small-object clients. Global improvement can therefore shift delay onto one client. We report per-client response time and hit rate alongside Jain's index rather than treating a single aggregate as sufficient evidence of fairness. [Per-client table](../data/results/main/per_client.csv) [Fairness table](../data/results/main/fairness.csv)

The fixed-`H` ablation replaces latency benefit, removes size normalization, or removes decay. Removing size increased response time by 2.75% on `repeated` and 1.45% on `mixed` relative to full RW-CSF, averaged over the capacity grid; removing latency benefit increased it by only 0.21% and 0.26%. Removing decay slightly *improved* the stationary main workloads (0.60% on `repeated`, 0.16% on `sequential`, and 0.01% on `mixed`), so those results alone do not support a claim that decay helps. [Ablation table](../data/results/ablation/ablation_table.csv)

A supplementary hotspot-drift experiment rotates the hot group every 480 requests. At 256 KiB, RW-CSF averaged 21.98 ms versus 43.14 for LFU and 42.05 for its no-decay variant; at 512 KiB the values were 9.69, 23.84, and 23.63 ms. However, at 128 KiB RW-CSF (61.22 ms) did not beat LFU (61.17) or no-decay (61.22). It also differed from GDSF by more than the pre-registered 3% bound at 256 and 512 KiB. **The pre-registered P1, P2, and P3 predictions are not all satisfied; we do not recast this supplementary result as a confirmed main hypothesis.** [Drift protocol](../d_experiments/DRIFT_PREREGISTRATION.md) [Drift table](../data/results/drift/drift_table.csv)

## 5. Limits, reproducibility, and conclusion

The workloads are synthetic and the cache model omits expiry and HTTP Cache-Control. The 44 MiB video always bypasses the cache and strongly affects byte hit rate and origin traffic; the nearly all-new sequential trace offers little opportunity for any policy. RW-CSF assumes the strategy's 5 ms latency matches the engine and cannot reject a cacheable admission. Tuning compared only four `H` values on one synthetic seed; their tuning scores were close, so `H = 20` should not be interpreted as a broadly optimal constant. Fairness is measured after the fact rather than encoded in the policy.

The reproducible entry point is `python d_experiments/run_all.py` from the repository root. It runs tuning, main comparison, fairness analysis, ablation, drift, and plotting into ignored `data/experiments/run_all/`; committed tables, manifests, and six figures remain under `data/results/`. Each manifest records seeds, capacities, policy parameters, code/source hashes, dependency versions, input hashes, planned/completed counts, and run status. An independent audit found complete condition grids and matching source hashes; raw per-run event files are regenerable but not all committed. [HOWTO](../HOWTO.md) [Audit](results-audit.md)

**Conclusion.** RW-CSF can reduce latency when protecting small, costly-to-fetch objects, but GDSF or LFU wins particular workload/capacity/metric combinations. Its gains can disadvantage media clients and do not guarantee reduced origin traffic. The appropriate policy choice therefore depends on the objective and the expected access pattern, not a single overall ranking.
